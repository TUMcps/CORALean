# Taylor Models and Other Validated Functional Inclusion Methods

K. Makino and M. Berz. "Taylor Models and Other Validated Functional Inclusion Methods". International Journal of Pure and Applied Mathematics, 4(4):379-452, 2003.

**Source:** https://www.ijpam.eu/contents/2003-4-4/2/2.pdf (the publisher's PDF, 74 pages, read in full; text extracted with `pypdf`. The PDF's header and the journal's table of contents both give pp. 379-452. The authors' copy at bt.pa.msu.edu refuses automated access and was not used.)  
**Inventory:** confirmed (the numbered items are Definitions 1-8, Theorems 1-3, Remarks 1-8, Algorithms 1-8, equations (1.1), (2.1)-(2.9), (3.1)-(3.2), (5.1)-(5.5) and Table 4; there are no Lemmas, Propositions or Corollaries)

The paper behind CORA's `@taylm`: a Taylor model is a polynomial plus a remainder interval
enclosing a function, closed under the arithmetic and the intrinsic functions, with a remainder
that shrinks with the `(n+1)`-st power of the domain. The arithmetic and intrinsics are
`ContSet/Taylm/`; this page adds the source's own notion of a Taylor model (`IsTaylorModel`), its
scaling theorem (`Scaled`), the code-list theorems and range bounding (`CodeList`, `codeEval`,
`rangeBound`), the multiplicative inverse, the inverse square root and the antiderivative. The
second half of the paper compares Taylor models with centered and mean value forms, interval
automatic differentiation, boundary arithmetic and ultra-arithmetic, mostly by experiment.

Def. 3 lists thirteen operations, each with its own expansion and condition; each is a row
below, `Def. 3 (exp)` and so on. The source's `D` and `x₀` are read in the normalized coordinates
its own implementation stores (Section 3: "normalized to the interval `[-1, 1]` with expansion
point `0`"), which are also CORA's and this tree's.

## Findings

**`Def. 3 (arcsin)`'s addition formula needs more than the range in `(-1, 1)`.** The source
rewrites `arcsin f = arcsin c_f + arcsin g`, `g = f√(1 - c_f²) - c_f√(1 - f²)`, under the sole
condition `B(P + I) ⊂ (-1, 1)`. `g` is `sin(arcsin f - arcsin c_f)`, so the identity is
`arcsin(sin θ) = θ`, which holds exactly when `|θ| ≤ π/2`
(`Taylm.arcsin_eq_add_arcsinShift_iff`). A range inside `(-1, 1)` does not give that: at
`c_f = -√3/2` and `f(x) = √3/2`, both inside, the angles are `∓π/3`, `g = √3/2`, and the right
side is `-π/3 + π/3 = 0` where the left is `π/3` (`Taylm.arcsinShift_counterexample`). Taylor
models of small width are unaffected (`f` near `c_f` makes `θ` small), which is presumably why
the condition went unstated; `Taylm.asin` expands at `c_f` without the formula.

**`Def. 3 (arctan)`'s addition formula needs `1 + c_f·f > 0`.** `arctan f = arctan c_f +
arctan((f - c_f)/(1 + c_f f))` is stated with no condition, and is Mathlib's `arctan_add` at `f`
and `-c_f`, which asks `f·(-c_f) < 1` (`Taylm.arctan_eq_add_atanShift`). Without it the formula
is off by `±π` or undefined: at `c_f = √3`, `f(x) = -√3`, `1 + c_f f = -2`, `g = √3`, and the
right side is `2π/3` where the left is `-π/3` (`Taylm.atanShift_counterexample`).

**`Def. 3 (antiderivation)` multiplies by the width where it needs the signed lengths.** Eq.
(2.7) bounds the remainder of `∫₀^{xᵢ} f dxᵢ` by `J·(bᵢ - aᵢ)`, `J = B(P - P_{n-1}) + I`. The
integral of a function with values in `J` over a segment of signed length `ℓ = xᵢ - x₀ᵢ` lies in
`ℓ·J`, and `ℓ` ranges over `[aᵢ - x₀ᵢ, bᵢ - x₀ᵢ]`, which contains negative lengths whenever the
expansion point is not the left end. `J·(bᵢ - aᵢ)` contains `ℓ·J` for those only when `J` is
symmetric about zero. In one variable on `[-1, 1]` at `x₀ = 0` and order `1`, `f(x) = 1 + x²` has
the Taylor model `P = 1` (its first-order Taylor polynomial), `I = [0, 1]`; the printed rule
gives `(x, [0, 2])`, while `∫₀ˣ f = x + x³/3` is `-4/3` at `x = -1`, below `-1 + 0`
(`Taylm.antiderivPrinted_counterexample`). `Taylm.antideriv` multiplies `J` by `[-1, 1]`, giving
`[-max |J|, max |J|]`, and encloses the antiderivative (`Taylm.antideriv_outer`).

**`Thm. 1` bounds the remainders only, and the products need the polynomials bounded too.** The
hypotheses are `f, g ∈ Cⁿ⁺¹(D)` and remainders `O(hⁿ⁺¹)` of models around `x_h`, with `x_h` free.
`I_{f·g} = B(Pₑ) + B(P_f)·I_g + B(P_g)·I_f + I_f·I_g` is `O(hⁿ⁺¹)` only if `B(P_f)`, `B(P_g)` stay
bounded and `Pₑ`'s coefficients do, that is, if the Taylor coefficients at `x_h` are bounded.
With `D = ℝ`, `n = 1`, `f(x) = x²`, `g(x) = x` and `x_h = 1/h`, both remainders are `O(h²)`
(`[0, h²]` and `0`) but `B(P_g)·I_f` alone has width about `x_h h² = h`. `Taylm.times_scaling_counterexample` proves it: the product's upper remainder is at least `h`, not
`O(h²)`. Read with `x_h` fixed, as
the proof's "follows directly" intends, the theorem holds; `Scaled` makes the bound on the
polynomial explicit, and the formalization is non-asymptotic: at every `h ≤ 1` the result's
constant depends on the inputs' constants and the order only.

**`Thm. 2` and `Thm. 3`: a code list defined over the range need not execute.** Both theorems ask
of `g` only that it be defined over the range of (an enclosure of) `P_f + I_f`. The intrinsics of
`Def. 3` carry conditions on the model they are handed, `B(P + I) ⊂ (0, ∞)` for `log`, and an
intermediate model's bound is typically wider than its function's range. For `g(y) =
log(y² - 2y + 1.01)`, defined everywhere, the argument's model on `[-1, 1]` is `x² - 2x + 1.01`
with interval-evaluated bound `[-0.99, 4.01]`, so `log`'s condition fails although the argument
is positive throughout. The formalizations state the theorems for a run that returns a model,
and `codeEval` returns `none` exactly where an intrinsic's condition fails.
